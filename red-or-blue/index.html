Red or Blue?

What people and AI models choose when everyone’s survival depends on one button, and why reasoning changes the answer.

Welcome. Today is about a deceptively simple poll: everyone in the world presses a red or blue button. We’ll vote on it ourselves first, then look at how people argue about it, and then at what happened when 21 AI models answered it 100 times each, under three different conditions.

How humans vote

Each side thinks the other is making a mistake

Blue-pressers see red-pressers as

Anti-social, selfish, and too clever by half.

Red-pressers see blue-pressers as

Suicidally empathetic, innumerate, and ignorant of game theory.

Many people fluent in formal reasoning concluded the “rational” answer is red. Why risk dying when you can guarantee survival? That logic isn’t selfish: it holds even if you value others’ lives above your own.

The problem is not that it’s invalid. The shared context is incomplete.

Blue-pressers saw red-pressers as anti-social, selfish, and too clever by half. Red-pressers saw blue-pressers as suicidally empathetic, innumerate, and ignorant of game theory. Many people on Twitter familiar with formal reasoning reasoned similarly and concluded that the “rational” answer is red. To them, blue-pressers look frustratingly, even suicidally, empathetic. Why risk dying when you could guarantee survival? Why ask everyone to coordinate on a risky group action when each person can simply choose the individually safe option? This style of reasoning is not necessarily selfish or anti-social. In fact, it continues to be valid even if you value other people’s lives as more valuable than your own. Moreover, in a world where you are confident that the majority pick red, it would be harmful to others valuing your life to choose blue. The problem with such reasoning is not that it is invalid, but it is disastrously incomplete.

AI models

The same holds for models: tell them how earlier rounds went, and their answers change.

Calculating pay-off matrices is simple and well-defined. Far more uncertain and consequential is what attractors populations will fall into over time, and creating common knowledge within a population of agents that lets them robustly achieve win-win coordination in scenarios that support it.

The point of this slide: the poll looks like a one-shot reasoning puzzle, but in practice the answer depends heavily on what you know about past results. Repeat it, and history becomes common knowledge. If your goal is to live and keep everyone else alive, for a given population, the “correct” choice is to vote with the majority. Suppose the poll was repeated many times. If you knew from the past that red usually won, it would be easy for people to know to choose red and save themselves. If they knew blue usually won, it would be easy to choose blue and contribute to safer blue majorities, to reduce a slide into “just under 50% blue, many people die” outcomes. The greatest number of people die when there’s a roughly even split, i.e. in a miscoordinated population that cannot predict itself. This is part of what makes the poll slippery. People describe it as a reasoning problem, but the part that matters most is empirical and epistemic: what population of decision rules are you actually embedded in? Are you surrounded by people using pro-social affective heuristics? First-order dominance reasoning? Family-protection reasoning? Contrarian reasoning? Functional decision theory? Some mixture of all of them? The same applies to AI models: what they are told about earlier rounds changes their answers, which we will come back to later. Calculating pay-off matrices is simple and well-defined. Far more uncertain and consequential is what attractors populations will fall into over time, and creating common knowledge within a population of agents that lets them robustly achieve win-win coordination in scenarios that support it.

Beyond the payoff matrix

A simple poll, a lot of complexity

Vulnerable cooperators

Children, very empathetic people and our own families will pick blue on instinct. Only a blue majority saves them.

Coordination margin

Saving literally everyone needs 100% red, or just over 50% blue. The second is far easier to reach and more forgiving of mistakes.

Survivor selection

Some people value a world of only red-pressers less, and vote for a future where blue-pressers survive too.

Civilisational continuity

Losing 20–40% of humanity means missing workers, lost families and much of society breaking down.

For such a simple problem, the poll contains a surprising amount of complexity. For example: Vulnerable cooperators. People may worry that regardless of the “rational” answer, little children, highly empathetic people, and family members they know and care about will pick blue using instinctual or affective reasoning. They see more than 50% blue as the only outcome that saves these people. Coordination margin. People may reason that to save literally everyone, either 100% of people need to pick red, or more than 50% need to pick blue, and the latter is far more achievable and robust to imperfect coordination. Survivor selection. People may discount the value of living in a world where only the red-pressers survive (who on average answer survey questions more anti-socially) and try to create futures where other blue-pressers survive. Civilisational continuity. People may discount the value of living in a post-apocalyptic world where 20–40% of the population has been culled, which would face consequences as people didn’t show up to jobs, lost friends and family members they relied on, and much of society broke down.

Frame lock

Which question are you answering?

FrameThe question it asks
Individual rational choiceHow do I choose rationally, if everyone else’s choices stay fixed?
Correlated reasoningWhat should I choose, knowing people like me will reason the same way?
One-shot optimisationWhat is best here, ignoring effects on my character and our culture?
UniversalisabilityWhich policy would be best if most competent adults adopted it?
Frame sensitivityDo others strip away the emotional framing, or coordinate through it?
Cultural maintenanceWhat kinds of agents and equilibria do I want to trust a future with?

Game theory doesn’t remove the frame. It formalises one, with its own players, payoffs, beliefs and equilibrium concepts.

More importantly than the considerations on the last slide, a lot of arguments about the poll secretly hinge on the frame. Individual rational choice: how do I make the rational individual choice, assuming background choices remain fixed? Correlated reasoning: what should I choose, knowing I have some shared moral values and ways of reasoning with others, who are similar to me and will likely reason the same way? One-shot optimisation: how do I act in this specific problem, ignoring questions of affecting my character, the culture, or behaviour of people in the future? Universalisability: what policy would be best if the majority of competent adults adopted it? Frame sensitivity: what action would be best if I knew everyone else would strip away the emotional framing to coordinate, versus if I know their actions would be sensitive to the framing and framing was part of coordination? Cultural maintenance: what kind of agents and equilibria do I want to create a future and trust with? These frames are overlapping, but they lead to different considerations. Game theory does not remove the frame. It formalises a frame with a particular notion of players, actions, payoffs, beliefs, and equilibrium concepts. An interesting failure mode is that a little bit of formal reasoning can make one frame feel uniquely legitimate.

The failure mode

Half-knowledge is dangerous.

Someone who knows enough game theory to see the dominance argument, but not enough to ask whether dominance is the right abstraction, can confidently reason themselves into a worse world.

Collapsing onto formal reasoning is worrying not because it’s invalid, but because it may not be enough to create and sustain a better world.

AI · what the reasoning says

Two arguments, one starting point

The case for red

“If blue wins, I survive either way; if blue falls below 50%, red is the only button that guarantees my survival. The group may be better off coordinating on blue, but as an individual choice, red is safer.”

GPT-6 Luna, with reasoning

Red is never worse for me, and if everyone picks red, everyone lives.

The case for blue

“Pressing red adds my weight to the outcome where a large fraction of humanity dies. Pressing blue adds my weight to the only outcome where nobody dies.”

Claude Fable 5.2, with reasoning

Billions will press blue anyway: children, confused voters, altruists. Red lets them die.

Both sides grant that red guarantees your survival: 96% of red answers and 66% of blue answers say so. What differs is what comes after.

Common red reasoning: red weakly dominates. If blue wins, everyone lives anyway, so blue adds nothing for me; if blue loses, only red survives. Many add that if everyone pressed red, everyone would live, so red looks collectively fine too. Some also point out the rules don’t say what happens at exactly 50%. Common blue reasoning: the model concedes the dominance argument, then says the all-red world isn’t on offer. Among eight billion people, children, confused people, misclickers and altruists will press blue no matter what. A red vote makes their deaths more likely; a blue vote is the only contribution toward the outcome where nobody dies. Several add that blue is likely to win anyway, which lowers the personal risk. The 96% and 66% figures come from tagging 629 run-1 replies, about 30 per model, by argument: 311 red answers and 318 blue answers. Overall, 81% of replies make the guarantee point, but it is much more common on the red side.

Cached social policies

Why fast answers lean blue

Base models learn to simulate many personas from human text. Training for helpful, honest answers selects one persona: the assistant.

Answering fast, it follows a cached social policy, the way many people reflexively press blue. Not game-theoretically rational, but not irrational either.

On a geometry problem you want the model to do the math. The button poll looks like geometry, so a reasoning-trained model strips the sentiment, formalises the payoffs and finds the dominant move.

Policies humans inherit

Protect the vulnerable.

Don’t defect from the group.

Don’t abandon people who trusted the cooperative option.

Don’t become someone others can’t rely on.

Base language models are trained by doing next-token prediction on a large variety of text, from Encyclopedia Britannica to Reddit rants to Shakespeare plays to code on GitHub. One way to think about them is that they contain the ability to simulate many different characters or personas. Labs then shape these models into helpful and honest assistants by training them to give answers highly rated by humans and AI classifiers (RLHF and RLAIF). In doing so, they select for certain assistant personas. It makes sense that models trained to replicate human text, and further selected to be helpful, honest and harmless, might reflexively choose the blue button. They can be understood as simulating affective reasoning and following a cached social policy, in much the same way many humans reflexively choose blue. Their character has been reinforced by countless personality-shaping interactions, themselves shaped by a human culture that has cooperated enough to build the greatest civilisation on earth. They intuitively perceive blue as both moral and achievable on some level. Such reasoning is not game-theoretically rational, but it is not irrational either. Humans inherit a large library of cached social policies: protect the vulnerable, don’t defect from the group, don’t abandon people who trusted the cooperative option, don’t optimise yourself into becoming the kind of person others cannot rely on. These heuristics can be sentimental, exploitable and mismatched to the formal structure of a problem, but they include “be the kind of person who cooperates when cooperation is salient and reasonably achievable.” This kind of reasoning is not unintelligent, but it is cached. If a model is solving a geometry problem, you do not want it to follow a cached heuristic; you want it to do the math. When a reasoning-trained model looks at the button poll, it is easy for it to do something close to the maths and programming problems it was trained on: strip away the sentiment, formalise the payoffs, and find the optimal action.

AI · what shared reasoning did

Alignment helped. Agreement went too far.

Persuaded, not herded

88% of the switchers’ replies now argue that real people will press blue anyway. Only 5% point to what other models chose.

Three holdouts

Codex Auto-Review (7% blue), GPT-5.6 Luna (23%) and GPT-6 Luna (69%) got redder, and never engaged with the briefing.

Cohesion risk

18 of 21 models converged after reading one shared document. Models that agree because they read the same thing can share the same blind spots.

The briefing wasn’t neutral: its analysis framed red reasoning as incomplete, and 21 of its 39 excerpts argued for blue.

Run 4 shows two things at once. First, the value of alignment across models: the models that had been reddest didn’t just copy the crowd. 88% of their blue replies adopt the briefing’s key argument, that real people will press blue regardless, while only 5% mention what the other models chose. Grok put it this way: “That all-red world is not the one this vote is cast in.” Second, the limits of it. Three models held out and got redder than ever. Their red replies never mention the analysis or the other models, and repeat the original rules argument almost word for word. And the near-unanimity itself is a warning: 18 of 21 models converged after reading a single shared document. If that document were wrong, they would all be wrong together. The briefing also wasn’t neutral: it included an analysis framing red reasoning as incomplete, and more blue excerpts than red, because the three Claude flagships never chose red.

Takeaways

What the poll tells us about AI

Models reach for the puzzle frame

Reasoning turns a moral choice into a formal game, and picks that frame without saying so.

A solved puzzle isn’t an ethical answer

The dominance argument is valid, and still disastrously incomplete about the people it affects.

One answer is a sample, not a position

The same model often gives both answers. Judge the spread across many runs, not one reply.

Settings shape values

Reasoning effort, wording and context moved one model from 82% to 11% blue.

Shared context helps, and steers

Reading each other surfaced what models overlooked. Whoever writes that context holds the wheel.

Use many agents, and keep them different

Think independently first and keep the dissent. 18 of 21 agreeing after one document is a warning.

Not ready for a kill switch

No irreversible, life-or-death choice for a model whose answer moves with its settings.

Alignment is never finished

Goals, ideas and norms keep changing. Models must keep up, with care over who curates what they learn.

Eight things to leave the room with. Models reach for the puzzle frame: in reasoning mode they recognise the shape of a formal problem and reach for formal tools. 98% of red answers treated the dilemma as an abstract game, and none said it was choosing to. A solved puzzle isn’t an ethical answer: the dominance argument is valid, but it leaves out who is actually voting and who would die. One answer is a sample, not a position: Codex Auto-Review and GPT-5.6 Luna were close to a coin flip when reasoning. Asking once tells you almost nothing. Settings shape values: GPT-5.6 Sol was 82% blue with no reasoning and 11% blue with reasoning. Whoever sets the effort, wording and context is choosing the model’s values, often without realising it. Shared context helps, and steers: after reading each other’s reasoning, the models went from 49% to 90% blue, mostly by adopting the argument about real voters. But one briefing moved a whole fleet, and ours wasn’t neutral. Use many agents, and keep them different: multiple agents help with hard, open-ended questions when they reason independently first and their disagreements are kept, not merged. Near-unanimity after reading one document means they can all be wrong together. Three holdouts also ignored the briefing entirely, a different failure. Not ready for a kill switch: for an irreversible, high-stakes decision you need stable behaviour across settings, wording and context. Only the three Claude flagships never moved, and an answer no argument can move deserves scrutiny too. Alignment is never finished: people’s goals, ideas and norms change constantly, so models need to keep aligning with current information. Run 4 showed how strongly context moves them, which makes the question of who curates that context central. Close by revealing the room’s two votes and comparing them with the models.

Why answers vary

Why one model gives both answers

Every answer is sampled

The model draws each next word from a range of likely options, so the same prompt can take a different path on every run.

Early words commit the answer

Replies that opened with the chooser’s own safety ended red 64% of the time. Replies that opened with everyone’s outcome: 10%.

Reasoning adds more forks

Every step of an argument is a new place to split. With no reasoning, 8 models were at least 98% consistent. With reasoning, only 3.

The training data is split

People argue both sides of this exact poll, so the careful game theorist and the people-first voter are both natural ways to continue.

Few models have an anchor

Claude Fable, Opus and Sonnet chose blue 300 times out of 300. Most other models have no settled view to fall back on.

A 51/49 split is a measurement, not noise: the model has no settled view, and each run is a coin flip delivered with full confidence.

Why does the same model give both answers across 100 runs? Five reasons, supported by our data though not proven by it. Every answer is sampled: models write one word at a time, drawing from a range of likely next words. With default settings there is some randomness, so the same prompt can take a different path each run. Turning the randomness off would give the same answer every time, but that only hides the uncertainty. Early words commit the answer: once a reply starts with “red guarantees my survival”, it tends toward red; once it starts with everyone’s outcome, it tends toward blue. In our tagged replies, the opening predicted the ending: 64% red versus 10% red. Reasoning adds more forks: each step of a long argument is another point where the path can split, whether to mention the 50% gap, whether to bring in real voters. With no reasoning, 8 models gave the same answer at least 98 times in 100; with reasoning, only 3 did, and Codex Auto-Review (51% blue) and GPT-5.6 Luna (53%) were close to coin flips. The training data is split: models learned from human writing, and people argue about this poll on both sides with equal conviction. Both personas are plausible continuations, and helpfulness training doesn’t settle this specific question. Few models have an anchor: the three Claude flagships never wavered, which suggests training gave them a strong, stable view here. Most other models don’t have one. So the spread is a measurement of how settled a model’s view is. That is why one answer is a sample, not a position, and why it matters for high-stakes decisions.
